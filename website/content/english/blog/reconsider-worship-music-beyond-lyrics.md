I'm glad we still sing together on Sunday. A room full of voices does something a playlist on my phone never quite matches.

I've led worship before - acoustic guitar, nothing fancy. I have also led **media** and built **worship slides every week**. So I'm not writing as an outsider. I'm the person who puts the lyrics on screen and gives the room a chorus to repeat. I kept doing the lazy check: read the lyrics, shrug, add the song. The words looked fine. I thought that was enough.

It wasn't. Not always.

A catchy chorus can pass a quick Bible check. It can still teach the wrong thing **after** the last chord. What we start to see as normal. What we point people toward. What someone searches for on the drive home.

Music sticks in your body before it settles in your mind. When I put a song in front of the room on Sunday, I'm not picking a mood for twenty minutes. I'm making a choice about what people carry into Monday. That is pastor work, even when it looks like a playlist.

Here is the frame I keep coming back to:

- **Song selection is teaching by repetition.** What you sing every week sticks.
- **The worship set is a message the church learns to trust.** People don't treat it like background music.
- **Putting a track in Sunday worship gives that ministry a voice in your highest moment.** No one may preach a sermon from that stage. The song still speaks.

If the lyrics are the only test, you are treating worship like wallpaper. You are not treating it like teaching.

The Bible pictures **Satan** close to God's glory **before** he fell - blameless in God's presence, then brought down by pride and violence. Scripture also warns that Satan can look like an angel of light.

**Beautiful is not the same as true.** Satan has never been neutral about what draws the heart toward worship. Neither should we be casual about what we lift in front of the flock.

That's why I don't treat a chorus as neutral. A song can repeat a view of grace all month. That view starts to feel like Sunday. The sermon may have said something else.

I hear two objections every time this comes up. I'll answer them first.

**Nobody is perfect.** I'm not asking for hymn writers with a clean past. Open an old hymnal and you're already singing words from messy lives:

- **John Newton** wrote *Amazing Grace* after years in the **slave trade** - buying and shipping people to be sold. His conversion was real. So was the harm he had already done. We still sing him because the lyric points to grace, not because his past was tidy.
- **Martin Luther** gave us *A Mighty Fortress Is Our God* and a Reformation the church still builds on. He also wrote brutal attacks on Jewish people later in life that I can't defend. The hymn survived because the **words** were tested and kept, not because we pretend the man was simple.

I'm not saying "only use music from those who have clean records." That would empty the room and make me a hypocrite.

**What follows the song matters more than a clean past** - not in a hymnal, not on a worship album. The gap isn't moral perfection. It's **what gets attached when the song leaves the building**. Newton's hymn doesn't open a feed of **writings defending the slave trade** on your phone. Luther's fortress doesn't play his worst writings next in the queue. A dead hymn writer, for all their mess, isn't running a **living ministry** that apps push all week.

Modern worship often is.

**That is why modern songs need a harder check than most hymns ever did.** The artists aren't worse people. The mess and the ministry are still **alive**. Open a streaming app and you're already one song away from a flawed human being:

- **The story can sell the song.** A moving testimony in the room. A lyric about Christ on the screen. Nobody checked whether the two matched until the chorus was already in rotation.
- **The poster can sell the singer.** Trust built on a face and a voice, not only on tested words. When private life became public news, the set list did not pause to wait.
- **The feed can outlast the album.** Clips, posts, and teaching from the same source keep reaching your people after Sunday. Sometimes they say something different than the four lines on the slide.

That ministry is still alive - website, feeds, sermon videos, posting this week. Your member can land there seconds after church. Nobody follows John Newton's new content on Tuesday. If you only read the lyric sheet, you are checking a dead page, not a **living ministry** people can reach the same day.

**It's just a concert.** I'm not saying pastors can't go. I'm saying a public post from the crowd is not a private memory.

If a pastor posts from a big worship night, the room may hear approval. The lights and crowd read like a public yes, louder than "I went to a concert."

When a leader posts from the crowd, the church can read: *this ministry is worth following*. Worth the selfie. Worth the public blessing.

That is why I compare it to the Sunday set list. I pull up a year of songs a church actually sang on Sunday morning. Fifty-two weeks. Not one snapshot.

**Sometimes the group's songs are barely there.** The concert got a public yes. A year of worship gave the group a quiet no. **Both are pastoral signals.** One is loud. One is quiet. Members often follow the loud one.

If you would not put that ministry on stage to teach, do not treat the caption like a private memory. The set list is the real answer.

**A famous Christian can send the same signal.** The opener isn't always a worship minister. Sometimes it's a mainstream singer people already know - faith on their public profile, but not someone your worship team would book for a teaching night. The ministry behind the event may teach salvation with extra conditions you would never preach. I don't know what that singer believes in private. I know what the slot did. The room heard *this ministry is safe* before the sermon. You don't have to preach to bless preaching. Showing up at their highest moment is enough.

If you're a pastor, what you preach on Sunday does not always match the set list or a concert post. Old hymnals **were tested over many years**. Spotify **suggests new catalogs right away**. When the post and the set list disagree, people notice - or they follow the loud signal.

The three risks below are what I watch for when I build slides. They show up when we treat an active ministry like a dead lyric on a page.

---

## When they follow up on their own

I've watched this from both sides: guitar in hand, and a laptop open for slides every week. Sunday ends. Someone from the church searches for the song.

The chorus was already in their head. That's the point. **A memorable song makes looking it up easy.** Nobody has to hand them a link. They remember enough to search on their own.

I do it too. Something moved me in worship. I open Spotify when I come home and save it to a playlist. Normal. Expected. Human. Nobody made me do it. I went looking on my own.

These groups don't sit on one track. They have a **deep catalog** - more songs, sermon videos, podcasts, books. The apps don't stop at what you searched for. They are built around **what the algorithm suggests next** from that catalog. Related artists. More worship from the same ministry. Teaching clips queued after the playlist. One tap and your member is listening to preaching you never checked - from the worship group that made the song.

That's the part I missed for years. I thought I was bringing in a chorus. I was also pointing people toward **ministries I never screened** - catalogs they would find when they looked further on their own.

Again and again I've heard the same path from people who later left teaching that hurt them. The **music came first**. They sat through a full sermon series from that ministry only later.

Someone loves a song on Sunday. They look up where it came from. They start following that ministry. Later they hear teaching that doesn't match what we preach. The song was the introduction. **Their sermons and feeds** were where the gap showed up.

When we sing a song at the high point of the week, we send a signal: *you can trust this*. People don't hear "the worship team liked this." They hear *my church approved this*. They assume someone with authority already checked the source. If we sing it, it must be okay - including whatever church and teaching network sits behind the group on the album cover.

I'm not saying every listener will soak up bad teaching all week. I am saying we pointed people toward teaching we didn't mean to bless - ministry content that doesn't match what we preach on Sunday.

The song might be clean. What sits behind it in the catalog often isn't.

---

## Same words, different meaning

Some teaching I won't put on stage doesn't start with obvious errors. It starts with **words we already use**.

Grace. The presence of God. Breakthrough. Glory. Words I use. Words in the Bible. Words that can mean something faithful in one mouth and something else in another.

When we repeat that phrasing in song, week after week, our people get used to the **sound** of the language. They meet the **teaching** behind it later. The terms start to feel like home. Familiar beats correct almost every time.

A good melody makes you less careful. You feel truth before you test it. That's part of why worship works. It's also how the wrong words slip in while everyone is still crying at the emotional part of the song.

Familiar worship words - grace, glory, breakthrough - can carry a whole teaching system when they come from the wrong catalog. I see it every week on the slides I build. The buzzwords look harmless in large type until you know what that ministry means by them. Music helps people remember what a sermon alone might not. If you don't know that ministry's vocabulary yet, the line looks harmless.

I learned this the hard way on a song I almost kept. The lyric sheet was fine. The hook kept saying "breakthrough" like a promise you could schedule. We weren't teaching that from the preaching on Sunday. I caught it late - on the third run-through, not the first read. I had sung along in other churches before, with no idea who sat behind the song. Plenty of people in the room are in that same spot every week.

Later, when the same words show up in a sermon clip the app suggested at lunch break, they don't sound like a warning. They sound like **Sunday**.

I've watched smart believers accept teaching they would have questioned on a printed page because they had already sung the chorus with their eyes closed.

---

## Good lyrics, wrong source

A song can match the lyric sheet and still act like a **hidden door** for a ministry I'd never invite to preach.

Using their music tells your church that source is acceptable, maybe even good. I think about that when I approve a slide: the congregation hears a louder message than the four lines on screen - *this source is okay*. The song can **make that ministry feel church-approved** and undercut any careful warning you gave from the pulpit.

Harmless-looking lyrics can still bless the house that released them. That house may still post teaching your people can tap into on Tuesday.

The name rides in on a melody people already love. Accept the music, and people start to accept the source. It creeps in slowly. First it's one song. Then it's the whole album. Then it's more from the same house - "since you already love the worship."

None of that showed up in the four lines we put on screen.

I think about people who are trying to heal after teaching that hurt them. They walk into a careful congregation and hear the same vocabulary they were trying to leave behind - same words, same emotional shape, different room. That is not a small wound. We can call it a stumbling block or we can call it careless. Either way, it is real.

Leaders land in a hard spot. How do you warn the church about a ministry's teaching while that same ministry's music lifts your highest worship moment? Mixed signals are not a small problem. They make it harder to **check whether what people find later still matches what you preach**, or quietly teaches something else.

I've been on teams that preached careful teaching on Sunday and sang careless paths on Wednesday practice. The congregation isn't stupid. They notice the gap even if they can't name it. When they can't name it, they usually pick what felt powerful in the room. They see the concert post. They hear the silence on Sunday. They fill in the blank themselves.

---

## The test I'd want a pastor to run

I still love Sunday worship with the whole church. I'm not calling for nothing but old hymns, and I'm not asking every church to copy my filter.

A pastor's job is to **protect the flock** - not with fear, not by banning every new song, but by thinking past Sunday morning. That protection is about **truth** - what you actually teach, not what you hope people remember. If you already think hard about what goes out from the pulpit, song selection is the same job on a different clock. A catchy chorus can send people looking further on their own.

**What they look up after Sunday** is where a different message often lands. That ministry is still posting while you are responsible for what your people hear. Song selection is part of flock protection, whether or not it feels as heavy as writing a sermon.

I am asking us to stop treating lyric checks as the whole job. Lyrics are step one.

**Step two is checking the ministry behind the song.** Modern worship ties you to groups that are active right now. They have websites, channels, and feeds your people will actually visit. On weeks when I'm building slides, I have time to read every line three times. The lazy check hurts when the words are already on screen and the room is about to sing them.

If you're a pastor, or you work with one on song selection, here is the filter I'd run before anything lands on Sunday:

1. **Find the ministries behind the group.** Not the artist's Instagram - the church, the network, the teaching arm that owns the catalog.
2. **Watch their messages.** Pick a few sermons or teaching videos. Sit with what they actually preach, not what the chorus claims.
3. **Ask the plain question:** Would I let this ministry speak in my church? Would I put them on the stage with a microphone and hand them my congregation for forty minutes?

Often, when you run that filter honestly, the answer is no. When it is no, that should stop you cold.

**The senior pastor almost certainly approved the songs that group released.** Worship teams don't float in a bubble. What ends up on the album went through leadership at that church. When you sing their track on Sunday, you're not importing a neutral tune from nowhere. You're importing a ministry you would not invite to preach.

I wouldn't book their worship team to lead a teaching night in my building. I wouldn't hand them the pulpit. So why would I let their song stand in front of the room at the highest moment of the week? The song does the introducing. Members can dig into that ministry's sermons and feeds on their own.

**Protecting the flock means taking that search path seriously.** Not every member will look further. Some will. They will assume you already checked.

Before I add a track, I still ask:

- **Where does this live?** What teaching network sits behind the label, the channel, the suggested feed?
- **What gospel does their teaching preach?** Not only whether one line sounds off. Does the ministry behind the song teach what **your church teaches on Sunday**? When I run this filter for myself, I ask one more thing. Does their catalog still teach **eternal life by faith in Christ alone**? Or does it add extra conditions I would never preach from the pulpit?
- **What words are we repeating?** Grace, breakthrough, glory - do they match what we teach, or load familiar words with a different gospel?
- **Would I hand this ministry a microphone?** If the honest answer is no, I need a better reason than "the key change hits."
- **Who might this hurt in the room?** Not everyone is new to the faith. Some people are recovering from bad teaching. Same song, different scar.

Sometimes the answer is still yes. Plenty of songs survive that test. More than I assumed when I only read lyrics.

## What people push back on

**They might say:** "The lyrics are biblical. The Spirit led us to this song."

**I'd reply:** I'm glad a song moves the room. Spirit-led doesn't end the job for me. Lyrics are step one. I'd still ask what ministry sits behind the track and what they preach when someone looks it up on Tuesday. I wouldn't hand a guest speaker forty minutes with a mic on the strength of a chorus alone.

**They might say:** "Look at the fruit - people are saved, healed, set free."

**I'd reply:** I don't doubt God can work in a room. **Moved** isn't the same as **taught well over time**. A powerful night doesn't answer what the ministry behind the song preaches in the sermons your members will find next.

**They might say:** "Don't touch God's anointed. You're attacking what God is doing."

**I'd reply:** I'm not going after a person. I'm asking whether a living ministry should get our highest-trust moment on Sunday. I'm also asking whether a pastor's concert post should say **yes** when a year of worship quietly said **no**.

**They might say:** "Worship isn't about doctrine. It brings unity."

**I'd reply:** I already said it above: what we repeat on Sunday is teaching. Some members will go deeper into the ministry behind the song on their own. That's not neutral unity.

**They might say:** "You're being legalistic / divisive / quenching the Spirit."

**I'd reply:** I'm not calling for no modern worship. I'm calling for the same check we'd give a guest speaker. Find the ministry behind the group. Watch a few messages. Ask if you'd let them teach your church.

**Protecting the flock isn't fear.** It's knowing a catchy chorus can outlast one sermon all week.

---

I'm grateful for teams that sweat the details nobody claps for - keys, transitions, slide timing, and teaching that doesn't end at the projector. Worship leadership is pastor work even when it looks like a playlist. It's media work too when you're the one who puts the words on screen.

If you're picking songs for a room you love, I'd rather lose a catchy chorus than accidentally say a ministry is safe.

That's the filter I'm keeping - care for people all week, not just the twenty minutes when everyone's standing.

I'm glad that care has earned its keep in the rooms I've served.
